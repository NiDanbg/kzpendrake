CHAPTER 1

Quibbletown had never passed for a place where you'd expect a dull day, but this morning the city exhibited an uncharacteristic restraint. It was as if it had had one too many the night before and was now pretending its memory was a blank slate. The streetlamps flickered hesitantly, the sewers hummed a melancholic tune, and the buildings around The Old Square—which usually glared at passersby with the snobbery of old aristocracy—today resembled hungover frat boys on the morning of a final. Pigeons landed and took off in suspiciously organized groups, and one little bird perched on the head of the Great Mage Quentis's statue was quite distinctly taking notes.
Wilfred "Willy" Spellburn slouched in the center of the square with his characteristic manner of a man trying to occupy less space than his physique required. He gripped his cheap magic wand, bought from a street vendor with the solemn promise that it was barely used. The instrument, slightly warped after the epic Chaos Maximus! spell cast against The Parking Meter two weeks ago, periodically sputtered sparks that smelled faintly of burnt toast. His robe, dyed in an indeterminate shade of wizarding gray, bore a prominent stain from a chocolate fountain—a memento from his previous disaster. The copper Honorary Defender of the City badge, its edge scorched as a souvenir of a dragonling's enthusiasm, hung crookedly on his lapel.
His hair—still the same bird's nest where someone had conducted a failed architectural experiment—suffered a new humiliation today. The feathered pedestrians were scrutinizing it with the critical fervor of a jury at an international competition.
"The socks," Willy muttered. "The important thing is wearing the right socks. The day I finally prove I'm not a total failure has to start with the right socks."
His choice, however, was disastrous. One was striped. The other featured embroidered dragonlings that were currently trying to escape the fabric, at least as much as their inherent two-dimensionality would allow.
Beside him, Mira Streetcaster observed the crowd with the eyes of a veteran who had seen things she'd much rather forget. Her cloak, equipped with so many pockets that she clinked like a hardware store during an earthquake with every step, gave her silhouette a distinctly martial air. Her hair was pulled back into a messy ponytail that looked natural in the same way the imperial palace looked "roomy." In her left hand, she held a standard wand, while in her right pocket, she concealed a relic-grade tool—no bigger than a pencil, emitting a soft, silvery light. This light remained invisible to anyone she didn't find sufficiently agreeable. At the moment, that applied to everyone present.
At Willy's feet sat the dragonling—a tiny, gravity-defying creature with chocolate smeared across its snout. Part of the sweet mess had dried up since the incident in the square, while the rest was a fresh coat applied this morning. It had tilted its head, tracking the pigeons with more intense focus than a starving cat. Its nostrils exhaled a thin stream of smoke in an unconvincing shade of pink.
Archibald had settled on Willy's shoulder—a former mage, a formerly dignified man, and currently a pigeon with an unusually piercing gaze. His feathers still bore the traces of last night's encounter with a piece of croissant and a pint of Interspecies Beer—a combination Archibald had dubbed "a well-deserved rest after saving the city." Up until now, he had been considered the only talking pigeon in Quibbletown.
"Something's off," Mira said quietly, stopping in front of the three parking spaces that, weeks after the incident, still vibrated with residual energy and attracted magical butterflies. "Do you see the pigeons? They were everywhere yesterday, and their behavior wasn't limited to just pecking."
"Pecking," Archibald noted with haughty disdain, "is beneath my dignity. It always has been. What these birds are doing is a ridiculous imitation of sentience."
Willy shifted his weight from the striped sock to the dragonling one. He was trying to summon the civic optimism befitting an Honorary Defender, but on an empty stomach, the feeling resembled a nervous breakdown.
"It might be residual fallout from The Parking Meter," he suggested cautiously. "Do you remember, Mira? After I overloaded the system with Chaos Maximus!, an anomaly appeared. Felix claimed it was attracting magical creatures. Maybe the pigeons got addicted to the background radiation."
Mira shot him a brief look of surprise. Before she could reply, one of the pigeons—larger than the rest, with feathers arranged in a mimicry of a lawyer's wig—stepped forward. It cleared its throat with a sound closely resembling "ahem," stood tall on its spindly legs, and spoke with the tone of a professor of ethics.
"Wilfred Spellburn, unregistered source of municipal disorder and accidental benefactor of the feathered intelligentsia! We, The Syndicate of Intelligent Pigeons, demand an immediate audience. We insist on the recognition of our legal status, the removal of all anti-roosting spikes, and a complete audit of the cornice allocations in the downtown district!"
Willy's jaw dropped, and his wand nearly rolled onto the cobblestones. Mira, who refused to be impressed by anything on pure principle, drew her tool with a smooth, menacing motion. The dragonling pinned its ears back and, with the deep respect of a predator encountering a brand-new type of prey, began searching for the source of the voice.
Archibald tumbled from Willy's shoulder, landed on the ground, and puffed himself up with such dramatic flair that two feathers fluttered down toward the dragonling sock.
"You are just birds!" he squawked, fighting with every fiber of his being not to sound like an ordinary pigeon. "You peck at crumbs, defile monuments, and build nests out of garbage! How dare you lay claim to intellect?"
The leader—who in the next two minutes would introduce himself as Sir Percival of the Cornice—glared at him with such utter contempt that even Inquisitor Grumble would have felt a twinge of professional respect.
"We have undergone an evolution, you transmuted fool. Thanks to the reckless spell of your friend here"—he nodded at Willy with a formal jerk of his head—"we have attained reason. Now we demand rights. Cornices in triplicate. The abolition of barbaric netting. And tax breaks for multi-brood families!"
The flock of pigeons began to chant in unison.
"Cornices! Cornices! No more nets!"
A passing citizen dropped his broom in sheer astonishment. A vendor, known as the quickest opportunist in the city, was already setting up a stall with a sign that read: "Amulets Against Talking Pigeons—Guaranteed Results or a Free Recast."
"Willy," Mira said, her voice dropping to a dangerously flat cadence. "What have you done this time?"
"I..." Willy gripped his wand so tightly his knuckles turned white. "It's probably because of The Parking Meter. Chaos Maximus! is powerful magic, Mira. The needle hit the Free Parking sector, but the excess energy must have dissipated into the local fauna."
"Free parking," Mira muttered, a hint of something akin to admiration slipping into her voice before she instantly squashed it. "Congratulations. You've gifted sentience to every feathered creature within a mile-and-a-half radius. So, what's next?"
"Rats demanding unions?"
From a nearby sewer grate erupted a deep, guttural squeak, followed by a rhythmic tapping reminiscent of a marching army wearing tight shoes. A delegation emerged from the shadows, led by a hefty specimen with a scar across its snout. Its tail swished with the professional authority of a union rep from a major dairy plant. The rodent stood on its hind legs, waved its tail like a conductor's baton, and spoke with the raspy voice of a street hustler who had spent thirty years peddling black-market curses.
"Spellburn! Streetcaster! We, the Sewer Workers Syndicate, Quibbletown-South Chapter, demand immediate negotiations! We insist on an end to lavender-based disinfectants, which leave entire families smelling like a garden party! Hazard pay for the night shift! Cheese must be recognized as legitimate legal tender for wages! And"—he pitched his squeak to a shrill falsetto—"at least one day off a week!"
The pack erupted into shrieks of approval.
That was a question, Willy whispered to himself. Not a proposal. It was a question.
"What's next, rats demanding unions?" he added aloud. "That was just a rhetorical question. I wasn't suggesting it."
"Next time, phrase your questions more carefully," Mira hissed through her teeth.
The dragonling, which had been observing the scene with uncontrollable digestive enthusiasm up to this point, dropped into a hunting stance and stepped forward with unmistakable intent. Willy, despite his shaky competence, managed to snatch it up just in time.
"No. No. No cheese for you. They have a..." He searched for a term the reptile might grasp. "...syndicate. We don't eat syndicates. At least not before noon."
The dragonling let out an offended puff of pink smoke and settled onto Willy's shoe, demonstrating its protest through absolute inaction.
Before anyone could react, a melodic voice, carrying the accent of an aristocrat educated abroad, drifted down from the canopy of an inspiring little oak tree.
"Actually, if you'll excuse me, we—the Confederation of Supreme Squirrels, Three Oaks Delegation—would also like to discuss a few details. The quality of accessible hazelnuts. The protection of tree canopies from unpredictable magical experiments. The expansion of the Thrown Nut Act. And"—the voice rose with genuine distress—"the resignation of that obnoxious black tomcat from the corner of Magic Spiral Street, who has been stalking our secretary for three weeks."
A dozen squirrels, lined up on a low branch like a parliament waiting for catering, bowed in perfect synchronicity. Their leader, sporting a glossy brown coat and the manners of a high-society dame, clutched an acorn in place of a scepter.
"This," Archibald noted while preening his feathers, "is unprecedented."
"This," Willy added, "is bad."
"This," Mira concluded, "is just the beginning."
The crowd of onlookers was already shifting from utter shock to Quibbletown's trademark entrepreneurial opportunism. Bartholomew Croissant, the baker—a man of such proportions that his every stroll down the narrow alleys constituted an "event"—was already handing out diplomatic crumbs to the pigeons. Sir Percival instantly declared this the first step toward interspecies diplomacy. Another vendor, whose stall had recently offered "Protective Runes Against Rodents," hastily rebranded his merchandise as "Translation Amulets—Find Out What Your Cat is Thinking (Warning: You Might Regret It)."
At that moment, the air shimmered with a familiar ripple, and the fountain in the center of the square shot up a jet that smelled unequivocally of chocolate. No one was surprised—ever since the last disaster, the monument suffered from periodic bouts of nostalgia.
"Mira," Archibald squawked, landing on Willy's shoulder with strained dignity, "I would like to point out that when you took on the task of saving the city from The Parking Meter, no one anticipated pedagogical consequences."
"No one anticipated them," Mira growled, "because no one expected Willy to drop Chaos Maximus! onto a device regulating the continuum between space, time, and fauna."
"I didn't know it controlled fauna!" Willy protested. "There was nothing about animals on the warning label."
"There wasn't a warning label on The Parking Meter at all," Mira pointed out.
"That's exactly what I'm trying to say!"
Archibald braced himself for a scathing retort, but a quiet footstep behind them made everyone freeze. The purr that followed was simultaneously melodic, commanding, and mildly insulting—an achievement Willy hadn't thought technically possible.
"We, The Feline Aristocracy," a black cat with eyes like polished emeralds and a coat that seemed to absorb the light said softly, "do not have grand demands. More time for sleep. A reduction of the canine population. Absolute jurisdiction over all sunbeams in the city. Fresh fish. And the condescending permission to continue tolerating your servitude."
The cat stretched out on a nearby bench with a grace that would have forced a ballerina into an immediate career change. The crowd gasped. An elderly gentleman cried out with the unbridled enthusiasm of a man in advanced dementia.
"That's my Murlinda!"
The cat shot him a look that made the man physically shrink back.
"I am Empress Mur," she stated icily, causing the rats to sink into a humble silence. "Not 'Murlinda.' I have never been 'Murlinda.' That name was forced upon me in my teenage years without consent, in direct violation of every natural contract between a feline and her enslaved human."
Willy allowed himself exactly three seconds to be impressed by the situation.
That turned out to be a mistake.
In the very next instant, pigeons, rats, squirrels, and at least one cat all started screaming at once—about cornices, about cheese, about hazelnuts, and about sunbeams. To Willy, it no longer sounded like an animal revolution, but rather a nightmarish budget committee meeting. And he, per tradition, was right at the epicenter.
"Willy," Mira said, her voice flat and exhausted, "welcome to the worst day of your life."
"It's not the worst," he objected. "Remember when I turned the fountain into a syrup geyser? The bees chased me for three straight days."
Mira looked at him with eyes capable of turning wine into vinegar.
"Do. Not. Remind. Me."
As she spoke, a pigeon swooped down from the sky. Its feathers were immaculately arranged like a dress uniform, and it landed with a theatricality that Sir Percival acknowledged with a slight nod.
"Sir Percival!" the newcomer announced with comical self-importance. "A message has arrived from the pigeons in Crooked Chimneys Village. They, too, are enlightened. And they insist on joining our syndicate!"
The crowd gasped again. These collective gasps, Willy noted, had become a mandatory rhythmic element of morning life in Quibbletown.
The dragonling glanced at the hat of a nearby mage, calculated the potential for arson, but postponed its intentions and curled back up on Willy's shoe with philosophical calm. Willy turned to Mira.
"So," he asked quietly, "what do we do now?"
Mira surveyed the square. The pigeons, the rats, the squirrels, and the smug cat. The fountain, which was undoubtedly spewing hot chocolate by now. The dragonling on Willy's foot.
"First," she said, "we determine the blast radius of this magic. And second"—her expression darkened—"we find Felix. Because if the squirrels from Crooked Chimneys Village have also started demanding floor time, we have a much bigger problem on our hands than I thought."
